0011  ## Abstract
0012  
0013  Reducing speckle measurements while retaining fine spatial detail is challenging in light-field microscopy. We present a mean-anchored self-supervised reconstruction network combining mean, variance, and raw-frame features with gradient-controlled statistical constraints. Training uses disjoint measurement subsets without three-dimensional ground-truth supervision; inference requires ten frames. In simulations, the network separated equal-width bars and gaps of 4.49 µm across ten subsets, whereas mean deconvolution consistently separated 6.73 µm features. Compared with a variance-anchored network, shape error decreased from 0.8169 to 0.7828. These results support ten-frame reconstruction of fine structures and motivate direct evaluation of resolution retention against higher-frame-count reconstructions.

0017  ## 1. Introduction
0018  
0019  Light-field microscopy (LFM) encodes spatial and angular information in a camera measurement and computationally reconstructs a three-dimensional fluorescent volume. Its acquisition geometry is attractive for volumetric imaging, but recovering fine structures requires inversion of a depth-dependent optical response. Wave-optics modeling and three-dimensional deconvolution provide a physical basis for this inversion, while subsequent developments have addressed reconstruction fidelity and spatial resolution in different LFM configurations. [1](https://doi.org/10.1364/OE.21.025418), [2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-023-01839-6)
0020  
0021  Spatially varying illumination offers an additional source of information. Under speckle illumination, different exposures modulate the same specimen with different intensity distributions. Blind structured illumination microscopy has shown that useful spatial information can be recovered without measuring each illumination pattern individually. Statistical approaches subsequently established the importance of illumination statistics and measurement covariance for recovering object information, and random illumination microscopy demonstrated their application to fluorescent biological samples. These studies motivate combining speckle illumination with computational volumetric imaging. [3](https://doi.org/10.1038/nphoton.2012.83), [4](https://doi.org/10.1109/TCI.2017.2771729), [5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.crmeth.2021.100009)
0022  
0023  The acquisition burden is a central consideration for such a combination. A reconstruction based on a sequence of random illuminations must extract reproducible structure from a finite collection of measurements. Increasing the number of exposures can improve estimation of the underlying statistics, but extends the acquisition interval when exposure and illumination-switching times remain fixed. For a changing or bleaching specimen, the assumption that all frames describe the same object also becomes more demanding. The relevant imaging objective is therefore to reduce the number of acquired frames while retaining spatial detail and maintaining structural fidelity. A reduction in frame count alone is insufficient if weak features disappear, nearby structures merge, or reconstructed detail is not supported by independent observations.
0024  
0025  Learning-based LFM reconstruction provides a route to incorporating priors across specimens. Supervised approaches, including VCD-Net and HyLFM-Net, demonstrated rapid volumetric reconstruction and approaches to validating the reconstructed signal. Learning from corrupted observations and physics-based self-supervision offer complementary strategies when clean target volumes are difficult to obtain. In particular, SeReNet demonstrated physics-driven self-supervised reconstruction for LFM, and recent work on unrolled blind-SIM integrated learnable reconstruction with unknown illumination patterns. Thus, ground-truth-free learning and speckle-based resolution enhancement already have substantial precedents. [6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-021-01058-x), [7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-021-01136-0), [8](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/lehtinen18a.html), [9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-025-02698-z), [10](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-68693-w)
0026  
0027  Here, we investigate how a shared reconstruction network can use a short speckle sequence to recover fine three-dimensional structure under a calibrated light-field forward model. Our design distinguishes the roles of the mean reconstruction, variance-derived information, and features extracted from the individual frames. The mean reconstruction provides the output anchor, while the other branches contribute complementary corrections. We further separate overall intensity from normalized structure: intensity is calibrated using the input measurements, and structural updates are driven by variance consistency together with a bounded contribution from mean consistency. This separation provides an explicit way to control competing statistical constraints during learning.
0028  
0029  The resulting method accepts ten speckle measurements for inference. During training, ten frames and their corresponding preliminary reconstructions form the input, while the remaining ninety frames of the same acquisition provide statistical constraints. No three-dimensional reference volume is used in the training objective or checkpoint-selection score. The main scientific question is whether this reconstruction strategy shifts the relationship between measurement count and recoverable spatial detail. We first establish its performance at the ten-frame operating point, including lateral feature separation, volumetric error, and stability across measurement subsets. We then specify the measurements needed to quantify resolution retention relative to higher-frame-count reconstructions and to validate the method in spinach roots and mouse brain sections.

0033  ## 2. Methods
0034  
0035  ### 2.1. Image formation and statistical measurements
0036  
0037  Let \(f(\mathbf r)\geq0\) denote the three-dimensional fluorophore distribution and \(s_n(\mathbf r)\) the illumination intensity in the nth exposure. Under linear fluorescence response, a discrete measurement is represented as
0038  
0039  \[
0040  y_n=\mathcal H(f\odot s_n)+\eta_n,\qquad n=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{acq}},
0041  \tag{1}
0042  \]
0043  
0044  where \(\mathcal H\) is the calibrated LFM operator, \(\odot\) denotes pointwise multiplication, and \(\eta_n\) accounts for measurement noise. The object is assumed to remain unchanged during acquisition of one sequence. Background correction and intensity calibration are applied consistently across frames. The statistical model below assumes that the mean illumination is spatially uniform over the modeled region or has been incorporated into the calibration.
0045  
0046  The implemented operator retains the periodic spatial dependence of the light-field point spread function (PSF). Object samples at different positions within a microlens period are propagated with their corresponding kernels and summed over depth. The selected PSF contains 49 × 49 within-period phase positions. These indices describe positions within a microlens period, rather than the number of microlenses. Preserving this dependence avoids replacing the light-field response with a single spatially invariant blur kernel.
0047  
0048  For a selected frame set \(\mathcal I\) containing N measurements, we compute the sample mean and unbiased sample variance,
0049  
0050  \[
0051  \mu_{\mathcal I}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\in\mathcal I}y_n,
0052  \qquad
0053  V_{\mathcal I}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n\in\mathcal I}(y_n-\mu_{\mathcal I})^2.
0054  \tag{2}
0055  \]
0056  
0057  The present reconstruction uses a diagonal, squared-PSF approximation to the variance response,
0058  
0059  \[
0060  \widehat\mu=\mathcal H(f),\qquad
0061  \widehat V\simeq\mathcal H_2(f^2),
0062  \tag{3}
0063  \]
0064  
0065  where \(\mathcal H_2\) is constructed by squaring each PSF kernel elementwise. It is not the composition of \(\mathcal H\) with itself. Equation (3) uses the intensity convention of the current implementation, which sets additional shot-noise and read-noise terms to zero. Spatially constant factors in the variance response are removed by the shape normalization described below. This approximation does not retain the full covariance associated with finite-width illumination speckles. The information-capacity results for full measurement covariance therefore cannot be transferred directly to Eq. (3). [4](https://doi.org/10.1109/TCI.2017.2771729)
0066  
0067  Two preliminary volumes are computed from exactly the same input frames. Mean-RL3, denoted \(M_{\mathcal I}\), is obtained by three Richardson–Lucy (RL) iterations using \(\mu_{\mathcal I}\) and \(\mathcal H\). Taylor-RL3-sqrt, denoted \(T_{\mathcal I}\), is the square root of the corresponding three-iteration reconstruction from \(V_{\mathcal I}\) using the squared-PSF model. The saved Taylor input is already in the square-root representation and is not square-rooted again during network inference.

0071  ### 2.2. Mean-anchored multi-branch reconstruction
0072  
0073  The network combines preliminary volumetric estimates with features extracted from the input speckle sequence (Fig. 1). Separate three-dimensional encoders process \(M_{\mathcal I}\) and \(T_{\mathcal I}\), using three feature scales with 16, 32, and 64 channels. Spatial downsampling is applied in the lateral dimensions, while all native axial samples are retained. Feature normalization in the volumetric encoders is separate from the physical intensity scale used in the output and forward losses.
0074  
0075  A third branch processes centered measurements \(d_n=y_n-\mu_{\mathcal I}\). The same two-dimensional convolutional encoder is applied to each frame, with 8, 16, and 32 channels at its three scales. The feature mean and standard deviation are aggregated over the frame dimension. The resulting two-dimensional features are lifted to three dimensions by combining them with the physical depth coordinates. This construction is invariant to permutations of the input-frame order and accepts an unordered sequence. The current model uses mean/standard-deviation aggregation rather than a Transformer.
0076  
0077  At scale l, the volumetric features form a joint feature tensor \(P_l\). The lifted set feature \(S_l\) is incorporated through a learned gate,
0078  
0079  \[
0080  F_l=P_l+\alpha_l G_l\odot C_l(S_l),
0081  \qquad
0082  G_l=\sigma\!\left(D_l([P_l,S_l])\right),
0083  \tag{4}
0084  \]
0085  
0086  where \(C_l\) and \(D_l\) are learned projections, \(\sigma\) is the sigmoid function, and \(\alpha_l\) is a learned scalar initialized to approximately 0.05. Each gate assigns one value per voxel, shared across feature channels. A three-dimensional decoder combines the fused scales and predicts a residual volume \(R_\theta\).
0087  
0088  The output is anchored to the mean-derived estimate. Defining \(A=\beta M_{\mathcal I}\), the nonnegative intermediate reconstruction is
0089  
0090  \[
0091  f_0=
0092  \begin{cases}
0093  A+R_\theta,& R_\theta\geq0,\\
0094  A\exp\!\left[R_\theta/\max(A,\epsilon_p)\right],&R_\theta<0.
0095  \end{cases}
0096  \tag{5}
0097  \]
0098  
0099  The decoder output layer is initialized to zero, so that the initial reconstruction equals A. The scale \(\beta\) is initialized by matching the forward projection of the mean anchor to the input mean and is constrained to a bounded interval around that initial value. Equation (5) permits positive corrections and nonnegative attenuation of the anchor; it does not impose a small-residual constraint. The Taylor volume remains an input feature when the reconstruction anchor is changed. The anchor comparison therefore retains the same types of input information in both networks.

0101  ### 2.3. Separation of intensity and structure
0102  
0103  We express the final volume as a nonnegative normalized structure multiplied by a global intensity scale,
0104  
0105  \[
0106  q=\frac{f_0}{\max(\sum_{\mathbf r} f_0(\mathbf r),\epsilon_q)},
0107  \qquad \hat f=a q.
0108  \tag{6}
0109  \]
0110  
0111  The initial intensity scale is estimated analytically from the input mean,
0112  
0113  \[
0114  a_0=\max\!\left(0,
0115  \frac{\langle\mathcal H(q),\mu_{\mathcal I}\rangle}
0116  {\max(\|\mathcal H(q)\|_2^2,\epsilon_a)}\right),
0117  \qquad
0118  a=a_0[1+0.2\tanh(\gamma)],
0119  \tag{7}
0120  \]
0121  
0122  where \(\gamma\) is a scalar shared across training examples. When calculating \(a_0\) and the mean prediction for the ordinary intensity loss, q is detached from the gradient graph. Consequently, that mean loss updates \(\gamma\) without directly modifying the normalized structure. At inference, the global scale is determined from the ten input frames and the learned scalar, with no access to additional measurements or a reference volume. The intermediate anchor scale \(\beta\) and the final intensity scale a have different roles.
0123  
0124  ### 2.4. Self-supervised statistical constraints
0125  
0126  For each training example, the acquired sequence is divided into disjoint input and constraint sets, \(\mathcal I\cap\mathcal C=\varnothing\), with \(|\mathcal I|=10\) and \(|\mathcal C|=90\). The input volumes and raw-frame features are derived exclusively from \(\mathcal I\). The mean \(\mu_{\mathcal C}\) and variance \(V_{\mathcal C}\) provide training targets in measurement space. This scheme uses additional training measurements but no three-dimensional ground-truth reconstruction as a supervised target.
0127  
0128  Let \(\mathcal N(u)=u/\max(\operatorname{mean}_{xy}u,\epsilon_N)\), and let \(\rho\) denote the mean SmoothL1 loss. The normalized variance loss is
0129  
0130  \[
0131  L_V=\rho\!\left(
0132  \log[\mathcal N(\mathcal H_2(q^2))+\epsilon_v],
0133  \log[\mathcal N(V_{\mathcal C})+\epsilon_v]
0134  \right),\qquad \epsilon_v=10^{-6}.
0135  \tag{8}
0136  \]
0137  
0138  The ordinary mean loss controls intensity,
0139  
0140  \[
0141  L_{M,a}=\rho\!\left(
0142  \frac{a\mathcal H(\operatorname{sg}(q))}{s_\mu},
0143  \frac{\mu_{\mathcal C}}{s_\mu}
0144  \right),
0145  \quad s_\mu=\max(\operatorname{mean}_{xy}|\mu_{\mathcal C}|,10^{-8}),
0146  \tag{9}
0147  \]
0148  
0149  where \(\operatorname{sg}\) denotes stop-gradient. A separate mean-shape loss contributes structural information,
0150  
0151  \[
0152  L_{M,q}=\rho\!\left(
0153  \mathcal N(\mathcal H(q)),\mathcal N(\mu_{\mathcal C})
0154  \right).
0155  \tag{10}
0156  \]
0157  
0158  Rather than using a fixed weight for this structural term, we limit its gradient contribution at q for each training sample:
0159  
0160  \[
0161  c_t=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[
0162  \min\!\left(1,
0163  b_t\frac{\|\nabla_q L_V\|_2}
0164  {\|\nabla_q L_{M,q}\|_2+10^{-12}}
0165  \right)\right],
0166  \qquad b_t=\min(t/50,1).
0167  \tag{11}
0168  \]
0169  
0170  Here t counts optimizer updates from one. The coefficient is detached, so differentiation through the gradient-norm ratio is not included in training. After the ramp, the weighted mean-shape gradient has a norm no larger than the variance gradient, subject to numerical tolerance. This is a bound in normalized-reconstruction space, rather than a bound on each network-parameter update. It does not require the two gradients to have equal norms or aligned directions.
0171  
0172  The full objective is
0173  
0174  \[
0175  L=L_{M,a}+L_V+c_tL_{M,q}
0176  +10^{-5}\operatorname{TV}_{0.5}\!\left(q\operatorname{sg}(a)\right),
0177  \tag{12}
0178  \]
0179  
0180  with
0181  
0182  \[
0183  \operatorname{TV}_{0.5}(u)=
0184  \operatorname{mean}|D_xu|+\operatorname{mean}|D_yu|
0185  +0.5\operatorname{mean}|D_zu|.
0186  \tag{13}
0187  \]
0188  
0189  The mean-shape gradient budget, referred to as “mean≤100%” in the experiment records, is therefore an adaptive optimization constraint, not a fixed loss weight of one. Self-supervised checkpoint selection uses the validation-object average of \(L_{M,a}+L_V+10^{-5}\operatorname{TV}_{0.5}\), excluding the training-only adaptive mean-shape term. The current implementation records ground-truth evaluation metrics separately; they do not enter this selection score.

0191  ### 2.5. Simulation data and implementation
0192  
0193  The current evaluation uses eleven training objects, three validation objects, and three test objects. Each object has one hundred simulated measurements and ten fixed input/constraint splits, giving 110 training, 30 validation, and 30 test object–subset instances. All measurements and derived volumes belonging to an object are assigned to the same partition. Different subsets of one acquisition can overlap and are treated as repeated measurements of the same object.
0194  
0195  Volumes contain 260 × 260 lateral samples and ten axial samples. The lateral pitch is approximately 1.12245 µm; depth positions extend from 10 to 100 µm in 10 µm increments. The configuration specifies detection and illumination numerical apertures of 0.15 and 0.05, respectively. These values characterize the present numerical configuration and are not substituted for unconfirmed experimental settings. The test objects comprise interrupted tubular structures (T02), a three-bar chart (T03), and axial pairs with control regions (T04).
0196  
0197  Both network anchors are compared after 400 optimizer updates using a common initialization seed of 20260901 and a global batch size of eight. The network is trained with Adam, using an initial network learning rate of \(10^{-3}\) and scalar learning rates of \(10^{-4}\). After 200 updates, these rates are reduced to \(10^{-4}\) and \(10^{-5}\), respectively. Validation is performed every twenty updates. The training contract records 1,188,377 trainable parameters and six participating GPUs. Computation uses FP32 with AMP and TF32 disabled. The final 400-update checkpoint is the primary model in the present comparisons.

0201  ### 2.6. Spatial fidelity, resolution, and measurement-budget evaluation
0202  
0203  Volumetric shape error is measured by an intensity-scale-aligned normalized root-mean-square error,
0204  
0205  \[
0206  \operatorname{NRMSE}_{\mathrm{shape}}
0207  =\frac{\|\alpha^*\hat f-f_{\mathrm{GT}}\|_2}{\|f_{\mathrm{GT}}\|_2},
0208  \qquad
0209  \alpha^*=\max\!\left(0,
0210  \frac{\langle\hat f,f_{\mathrm{GT}}\rangle}{\max(\|\hat f\|_2^2,\epsilon)}\right).
0211  \tag{14}
0212  \]
0213  
0214  The scale adjustment is used only for evaluation and does not spatially register the reconstruction. We additionally report the Wasserstein-1 distance between normalized axial intensity distributions, the structural similarity of lateral maximum-intensity projections (MIPs), and the fraction of reconstructed mass outside a lateral support mask. The latter mask is obtained by thresholding the ground-truth MIP at 10% of its maximum and applying two binary-dilation iterations. It is a lateral support measure, rather than a full three-dimensional background measure. Object-level values are averaged across subsets before equal-weight averaging across test objects.
0215  
0216  The T03 chart contains horizontal and vertical groups of three equal-width bars with equal-width gaps. Bar widths are 2.24490, 3.36735, 4.48980, 6.73469, 8.97959, and 11.22449 µm. For each group, evaluation selects the native plane having the largest reconstructed mass within its predefined region. The selected plane must lie within 10 µm of the target depth. A transverse profile is averaged over the central 60% of the bar length. All three detected peaks must match the target centers within two lateral pixels, exceed 10% of the full-volume maximum, and have intervening valley-to-weaker-peak ratios no greater than 0.8. The 5% and 20% peak thresholds are retained as sensitivity analyses. This criterion jointly evaluates localization, feature detection, and separation, so its aggregate success rate is not a pure optical transfer-function measurement.
0217  
0218  For subset stability, each reconstruction is normalized to unit mass. We calculate its relative deviation from the subset-average volume and the pairwise Wasserstein distance between subset axial profiles. For structural specificity, the tubular object is assessed using centerline localization, false breaks, and false connections across known interruptions. Axial pairs are evaluated on native depth samples without interpolation to create apparent intermediate peaks.
0219  
0220  To quantify measurement reduction, we define the required frame count for method m at a predeclared fidelity target \(\mathcal Q_*\) as
0221  
0222  \[
0223  N_{\min}^{(m)}(\mathcal Q_*)=
0224  \min\{N:\text{method }m\text{ satisfies }\mathcal Q_*\text{ with }N\text{ frames}\}.
0225  \tag{15}
0226  \]
0227  
0228  The target must include a spatial-separation or measured-resolution criterion together with limits on structural error and false features. The validated frame-reduction factor relative to a baseline is then \(N_{\min}^{(\mathrm{baseline})}/N_{\min}^{(\mathrm{proposed})}\). This definition prevents a nominal reduction in input count from being interpreted as a successful imaging improvement when structural fidelity is lost.

0248  ## 3. Results
0249  
0250  ### 3.1. Fine lateral structure recovered from ten measurements
0251  
0252  The three-bar chart provides a direct test of whether a ten-frame reconstruction preserves neighboring features at different lateral scales (Fig. 2). Under the predefined 10% peak threshold and valley criterion, the mean-anchored network separated the 4.48980 µm bar-and-gap groups in both orientations for all ten input subsets. The 3.36735 and 2.24490 µm groups were not separated. Mean-RL3 consistently separated the 6.73469 µm groups, while at 4.48980 µm it passed in none of the ten horizontal evaluations and one of the ten vertical evaluations. The proposed network therefore recovered smaller chart features more consistently than its mean-derived initialization at this ten-frame operating point.
0253

0304  
0305  Changing the anchor while retaining the feature branches, training data, initialization seed, and optimization budget provides a controlled test of the output parameterization. These changes show that the predicted shape and depth distribution became more consistent across the ten selected measurement subsets of each object.

Mean-RL3 had a smaller normalized subset dispersion of 0.1811 and a smaller pairwise axial distance of 0.038 µm, despite its substantially larger ground-truth shape and depth errors. This finding indicates that the learned correction contributes substantially to the final structure. Mechanistic interpretation therefore requires more than visually comparing the input and final images.

0331  ### 3.7. Acquisition and computational cost
0332  
0333  The proposed inference pipeline includes calculation of the frame statistics, two preliminary RL reconstructions, network evaluation, and intensity calibration. For large experimental fields, any tiling, context handling, and stitching contribute to the total cost. Consequently, network-forward time alone is insufficient to characterize end-to-end reconstruction throughput. Figure 3 or a supplementary timing table will report these stages separately alongside the acquisition duration.
0334  
0335  At fixed exposure and switching conditions, reducing the number of measured frames can shorten the interval required for one volume. The achievable improvement depends on the overheads and on the number of frames needed to meet the same spatial-fidelity criterion. Photobleaching and dose reduction also require their own exposure-controlled measurements; they cannot be inferred from the training input/constraint split.

0339  ## 4. Discussion
0340  
0341  This study targets the trade-off between the number of speckle measurements and the spatial information retained in an LFM reconstruction. The present ten-frame results provide evidence that the learned reconstruction can recover neighboring chart features more consistently than the mean-derived initialization while improving volumetric shape and depth-distribution metrics. The principal intended advance is measurement-efficient recovery of fine structure. Establishing the size of that advance requires the matched-fidelity frame-budget evaluation, rather than a comparison of nominal frame counts.
0342  
0343  The mean anchor and statistical gradient design address complementary parts of this problem. The anchor supplies a reproducible initialization tied to the input mean, while the Taylor and frame-set features allow the decoder to use information absent from that initialization. The intensity/structure separation prevents the ordinary mean data term from directly changing the normalized shape. The additional mean-shape term reintroduces structural mean information with an explicit gradient bound. The current anchor comparison supports its aggregate benefit in this implementation, but does not isolate the necessity of every branch or establish a general optimality property. In particular, the substantial effective correction and the continuity errors motivate the proposed ablations and independent structural validation.
0344  
0345  The relationship to earlier work is also specific. Speckle-based blind-SIM and RIM already exploit unknown illumination patterns and their statistics; learning-based LFM and physics-driven self-supervision already provide alternatives to clean volumetric supervision. Recent unrolled blind-SIM further demonstrates learned physics-based reconstruction in the unknown-illumination setting. The contribution investigated here is their use within a mean-anchored, multi-statistic reconstruction for a short sequence of light-field measurements, with explicit control over mean and variance structural updates. A quantitative comparison with another method requires a compatible optical operator, input budget, and training protocol, rather than transferring reported resolution or runtime values from a different microscope. [3](https://doi.org/10.1038/nphoton.2012.83), [5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.crmeth.2021.100009), [9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-025-02698-z), [10](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-68693-w)
0346  
0347  Several limitations determine the current scope. First, a three-bar separation result does not itself establish performance beyond the optical diffraction limit, and neither bar width nor reconstruction-grid spacing is equivalent to PSF FWHM. Second, axial-distribution improvement is distinct from axial two-feature resolution; the current pair-separation results remain unsuccessful. Third, the squared-PSF variance approximation omits finite-speckle covariance terms and needs validation against illumination, noise, and PSF mismatch. Finally, the main comparison uses one training seed and three development test objects, and the available biological transfer lacks an independent three-dimensional reference. The pending experiments address these limitations directly.
0348  
0349  If the proposed method meets the same spatial-fidelity target with fewer exposures in independent simulations and biological specimens, its practical value will be a shorter acquisition window for a given level of structural detail. The spinach-root and mouse-brain experiments are designed to test this proposition on different tissue organizations while checking for false structures. Broader claims about phototoxicity, dynamic imaging, or transfer across microscopes will require additional evidence appropriate to those applications.
0350  
0351  ## 5. Conclusion
0352  
0353  We introduced a mean-anchored self-supervised method for volumetric reconstruction from ten speckle-illuminated light-field measurements. The method combines mean, variance-derived, and raw-frame features with separate intensity calibration and gradient-controlled structural constraints. Existing simulations show consistent separation of 4.49 µm equal-width bars and gaps and improved aggregate shape and depth-distribution errors relative to a Taylor-anchored network. These findings support ten-frame recovery of fine structure under the tested conditions. The validated acquisition-reduction factor, experimental spatial resolution, and performance in spinach roots and mouse brain sections remain to be established by the measurements identified in this draft.
